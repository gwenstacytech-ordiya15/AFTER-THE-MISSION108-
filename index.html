01 · Interactive exhibit hall

Start with the Moon and Mars. Follow the evidence.

Begin with the Moon and Mars exhibits, then compare them with Saturn and deep-space missions. Each dossier separates the spacecraft's scientific achievements from the fate of its hardware.

Mission map

A simplified guide to the places in this museum—not a scale model.

Tip: the pins open a featured dossier. Use the exhibit cards to explore every spacecraft.

The big idea

A mission can end in more than one way.

Some hardware stays where it worked. Some stops communicating. Some is deliberately guided into a planet. Some spacecraft keep travelling long after their original planetary encounters.

Important distinction: “Abandoned” is not always the right scientific description. A spacecraft may be inactive, intentionally disposed of, still operating, or left in place as part of the mission design.

What to look for in every dossier

  • The spacecraft's job and major discovery.
  • How and when its mission ended—or whether it is still active.
  • Why the hardware was left, lost, or intentionally disposed of.

05 · Hands-on STEM activity

Build the shoebox space museum

Turn the digital exhibits into a physical display. The model should help visitors understand the different destinations and mission endings at a glance.

01

Prepare the space

Line the inside of a recycled shoebox with dark paper. Add small stars and clear labels. Keep the labels readable from the front.

02

Create the worlds

Make a grey cratered Moon, rusty-red Mars and a ringed Saturn. Use raised paper, clay or safe recycled materials for texture.

03

Add the spacecraft

Make small, recognisable silhouettes from paper or foil. Add mission names and numbered exhibit markers.

04

Show the endings

Use a simple legend: left in place, lost communication, planned disposal, or still travelling. Do not label every object “abandoned.”

05

Connect the website

Place a QR code next to each exhibit that links to its matching dossier. Test every code on a phone before display.

06

Present the story

Give visitors one question to consider: “If you designed this mission, what would you plan to do with the spacecraft at the end?”

Model accuracy note: the map and shoebox are educational illustrations, not to scale. Make that clear on the display.

08 · Test the museum in the real world

Does the story help someone learn?

A strong educational project should be tested with real visitors. Use this simple protocol during your demonstration and report only results your team actually collects.

01 · Before

Ask one question

“What do you think happens to spacecraft when their missions end?” Record the visitor's answer without names.

02 · Explore

Let them use the museum

Ask visitors to open one Moon or Mars dossier and complete the mission decision lab without coaching.

03 · After

Check what changed

Ask the same question again. Count how many visitors can now distinguish hardware left in place, lost communication and planned disposal.

Evaluation note: this page does not collect or transmit visitor responses. Keep your own anonymous tally and include the number of participants, method and date when presenting results. Never invent impact statistics.
